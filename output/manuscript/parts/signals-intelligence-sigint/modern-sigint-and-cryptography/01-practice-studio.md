#### Lesson 3: Encryption, Integrity, Authentication, and Lawful-Access Claims

**Concept.** **Encryption, Integrity, Authentication, and Lawful-Access Claims** frames encryption as a policy and assurance trade-off among confidentiality, lawful process, communications security, and trust.

**Worked reasoning and limits.** Confidentiality limits who can read content; integrity concerns modification; authentication binds an asserted origin to a credential/key under stated trust assumptions; provenance records the content's lineage; none proves factual truth. End-to-end encryption describes where content is protected and decrypted, not a blanket guarantee about compromised endpoints, metadata, integrity in every construction, or legal immunity. A bare hash only supports a comparison when the reference and comparison process are trustworthy; a signature requires a trusted key/signatory relationship and does not certify the statement's accuracy [@official_nist_fips_186_5_dss]. 'Lawful access' describes a legal/policy question, not proof that a technically secure access mechanism exists or that any named actor has authority. #### Lesson 8: Steganography detection-literacy and communications-security

**Concept.** **Steganography detection-literacy and communications-security** connects the technical term to authority, minimization, communications-security risk, and public doctrine rather than interception mechanics.

**Worked reasoning and limits.** Steganography aims to conceal the existence of a message within another carrier, whereas encryption principally protects intelligibility; neither property establishes the message's truth. LSB means least-significant bit, a low-order part of a numerical image representation; DCT means discrete cosine transform, a frequency-coefficient representation used in image processing, while network-layer approaches concern communication structures.
