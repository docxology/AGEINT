Lesson 1: The Cognitive Attack Surface: Attention, Memory, Trust, and Decision-Making

Concept. The Cognitive Attack Surface: Attention, Memory, Trust, and Decision-Making maps cognitive attack surfaces: attention, belief formation, narrative provenance, and transparent correction options.

Worked reasoning and limits. Attack surface is a defensive organizing metaphor here: competing demands may affect attention, repetition may affect familiarity, authority cues may affect trust, and incomplete information may affect decisions. These are hypotheses about processes, not observations of anyone’s private mental state. Ordinary error, satire, accessibility problems, and legitimate disagreement remain alternatives; the policy paper does not validate a detector for manipulation or person-level susceptibility [ageint095].

Why it matters. The Cognitive Attack Surface connects classroom vocabulary to Cognitive Security and Influence Resilience practice: learners document evidence, caveats, and reviewer ownership rather than repeating labels.

Lesson 2: Adversarial Cognitive Operations: Defensive Recognition and Attribution Limits

Concept. Adversarial Cognitive Operations: Defensive Recognition and Attribution Limits uses attribution indicators cautiously by separating technical similarity, context, confidence, and geopolitical inference.

Worked reasoning and limits. Historical and doctrinal terms describe different institutional practices and contexts; they are not interchangeable labels for every false post. Separate a content finding, a distribution pattern, and an actor-attribution claim, each of which needs its own evidence. Lesson 3: Deepfakes, Voice Cloning, and Digital Identity Attacks

Concept. Deepfakes, Voice Cloning, and Digital Identity Attacks focuses on transparent resilience education: provenance, audience harm, attribution caution, and non-manipulative response options.

Worked reasoning and limits. Completed synthetic media triage. At 10:00 packet M contains a fictional aquarium voice clip claiming tomorrow’s closure; M2 copies the same clip, a supplied detector flags it 0.82, and no provenance credential is present. The triage record separates media authenticity (unresolved), closure truth (unconfirmed), speaker authorization (unknown), and actor intent (unknown); neither a detector score nor missing credentials proves fabrication. Decision: preserve the supplied originals and metadata, group M/M2 as one origin, and hold both the closure claim and any public accusation. At 10:30 instructor card O, an independent fictional operations schedule, confirms a closure for repairs but does not authenticate the clip, so draft v2 corrects the closure fact while retaining all three other unknowns. No real contact, voice matching, identity search, automatic response, or dissemination occurs; generic software-security citations are governance context, while the domain anchors discuss deepfake risk and limitations [official_nsa_cisa_contextualizing_deepfake_threats; scholarly_cardenuto_2023_synthetic_realities].

Lesson 7: Cognitive Security and Active Inference: Modeling Perspective

Concept. Cognitive Security and Active Inference: Modeling Perspective treats active inference as an agent-modeling vocabulary: beliefs, actions, expected observations, and policy selection are classroom concepts, not proof that an intelligence agent should act autonomously.

Worked reasoning and limits. Active inference supplies a vocabulary of generative models, beliefs, observations, and policies for studying perception and action. A human analogy, a thesis review, and an implemented agent model are different evidence classes; a coherent explanation does not establish that a particular person’s beliefs were manipulated or that an AI intervention is safe. Keep model assumptions explicit and do not derive persuasion tactics, clinical claims, or human-agent equivalence from the vocabulary [ageint106; ageint003].
